How the comparison is run

  1. Normalise the horizon. Pick one holding period (Q-25) and apply it to both cases. Comparing a ten-year hold to a sale today is the most common error in this analysis.
  2. Build after-tax cash flows for both. Hold: rent minus unreimbursed costs minus tax on income, plus terminal value net of tax. Sell: net proceeds after tax and transfer cost, invested at a stated rate.
  3. Discount both at the same rate. Use the Treasury plus a disclosed spread, and show the sensitivity to ±100 bps. A hold case that wins only at a 6% discount rate has not won.
  4. Stress the renewal. Run 100% renewal, 50% renewal, and vacancy-then-re-tenant. The spread between those three is the real risk in the hold case.
  5. Stress the black swans together. Wildfire, earthquake and flood do not arrive independently of each other's consequences — see the risk register.
  6. Declare a winner only when every gate is green. Until then, the output is a range, and the range is printed as a range.

The one structural point that survives missing data

Selling converts a deferred tax into a paid tax; holding keeps it deferred. Under the verified rules on the after-tax page, a sale at these rates can consume roughly a fifth of the price in tax and transfer cost in the illustration, and more where the basis is lower. That does not mean “never sell” — it means the sell case has to clear a higher bar than the raw price suggests, and that a 1031 exchange or a hold-until-basis-reset strategy belongs in the conversation before any listing. Conversely, holding keeps capital concentrated in one asset in one seismic region. Both of those statements are true at once, which is why the answer is a balance, not a verdict.
