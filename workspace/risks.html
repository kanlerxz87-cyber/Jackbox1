Black swans: why they are modelled together

A natural disaster damages a landlord through three channels at once, and the three reinforce each other: rent stops (business interruption ends before repairs finish), repairs are unbudgeted (and the lease may not allocate them), and exit liquidity disappears (buyers vanish exactly when you would want to sell). Modelling them separately understates the loss, which is why the stress scenario in the calculator applies all three simultaneously.

Channel 1 · Loss of rent

Rent abatement during restoration, business-interruption coverage that runs out early, and a tenant who may not reopen at all. A NNN lease does not protect against this — it protects against operating cost surprises, not against the building being unusable.

Channel 2 · Repair cost

Seismic retrofit, structural repair, and code upgrades triggered by reconstruction. Confirm who pays under the lease and whether the insurance limit is realistic against current construction costs.

Channel 3 · Exit liquidity

After a regional event, buyers reprice or disappear. The exit assumption in the hold case is the assumption most likely to be wrong in exactly the year it matters.

How the risk balance is meant to be read Every mitigation above is cheap relative to the loss it prevents. That is the actual recommendation of this page: not “sell” or “hold”, but “spend two weeks and a few thousand dollars closing the gates before spending a decision on it.” The calculator lets the owner see what the black-swan case does to each option before any of that money is committed.
