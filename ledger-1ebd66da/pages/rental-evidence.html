W-07

Pricing the short-lease discount from real quotes

This is the number the whole decision turns on. A buyer of a single-tenant NNN asset is buying a lease, not a building — and a lease with four years left is worth materially less than the same lease with twelve. That discount is not theoretical and it is not a modelling choice: it is observable in closed trades. This page sets out how to measure it properly, what is already in hand, and what must be bought.

No cap rate on this page. The brief asked to price the discount from real quotes. No official statistical series publishes submarket NNN retail capitalisation rates, and commercial data providers are proprietary. Publishing a plausible-looking number here would be the single most damaging thing this workspace could do, because a wrong cap rate silently corrupts every downstream calculation. FLAG-U04 →

Reading the two anchors honestly

The 10-year Treasury at 4.92%. This is the risk-free rate a buyer compares against. It tells you the floor under any required return — nobody buys an illiquid single-tenant asset to earn less than a Treasury. It says nothing about the spread a buyer will demand.

The tenant's own 6.1% lease discount rate. This is Jack in the Box's weighted-average rate for its operating lease liabilities — its own cost of lease capital. A landlord negotiating a renewal is negotiating against a counterparty who books the obligation at roughly 6.1%.

Why they don't give you a cap rate. A cap rate is NOI ÷ price. Both anchors are rates, not prices, and neither reflects the specific property's location, condition, tenant credit at the store level, or remaining term.

What they do give you. A defensible bracket for the renewal negotiation: a rent that implies a landlord yield far above the tenant's own 6.1% book rate is a rent the tenant has a strong incentive to resist, and vice versa. That is a real negotiating insight derived from a primary filing.

2 · Why the discount exists — the mechanism, not a rule of thumb

Four separate reasons a short lease trades at a wider cap rate Each is a distinct mechanism with a different mitigant. Lumping them into one "short lease = X basis points" rule is how sellers get underpaid.
MechanismWhat the buyer is actually pricingMitigant that narrows it
Re-leasing risk The probability the space goes vacant, plus the downtime and tenant-improvement cost if it does. For a purpose-built drive-through this can be a long, expensive void. A renewal option at a known rent, or a demonstrated multi-tenant demand for the site.
Financing duration mismatch A lender will not write a 10-year loan against a 4-year lease. The buyer's debt is shorter and more expensive, which lowers the price they can pay. Term extension before marketing. This is usually the single largest lever.
Rollover capital The buyer reserves for leasing commissions and TI at expiry, and deducts that reserve from the price today. A tenant renewal that waives commissions, or a lease that puts renewal costs on the tenant.
Marketability to the buyer pool Buyers seeking long-income product — 1031 exchange buyers, conservative private capital, some funds — simply do not bid. Fewer bidders means a lower clearing price. Nothing except term. This one cannot be argued away.
The implication for timing. Three of the four mechanisms are functions of remaining term, and one is a function of the buyer pool. All four get worse as expiry approaches — and they get worse non-linearly, because buyers start excluding the asset from their search entirely once term drops below what their lender will finance. There is a cliff, not a gentle slope. Identifying where that cliff sits for this property is the most valuable single piece of research available.

3 · The comparable-evidence protocol

This is the work that replaces a guess. It is deliberately specific, because "get some comps" is not an instruction anyone can execute.

Required fields for each comparable trade

  1. Closing date and sale price — from the recorded deed or a paid transaction database, not a listing.
  2. Capitalisation rate on in-place NOI at closing — not a broker's pro-forma cap. If the actual NOI is not disclosed, the comp is of limited use and must be marked as such.
  3. Remaining lease term at closing, in years and months. This is the variable being measured, and it must come from the lease, not an estimate.
  4. Tenant identity and credit — corporate, franchisee, or guarantor. A franchisee-guaranteed lease and a corporate lease are different products.
  5. Rent per square foot and escalator structure — a flat lease and a CPI-linked lease at the same cap rate are not the same asset.
  6. Building size, land size, and drive-through presence — the physical features that determine re-leasability.
  7. Renewal options remaining and their rent structure.
  8. Financing — all-cash or leveraged, and at what loan-to-value. A leveraged comp at a short term tells you more than an all-cash one.

A comp missing items 2 or 3 cannot be used to price the term discount. Recording it anyway and averaging it in is how a discount gets measured wrong.

The regression that answers the question

With eight to twelve clean observations, regress capitalisation rate on remaining lease term and the physical controls. The coefficient on remaining term is the market's price of a year of lease — which is exactly the number needed to decide whether to sell now or extend first.

cap_rate = a + b · remaining_term_years
             + c · rent_psf
             + d · drive_through (0/1)
             + e · corporate_lease (0/1)
             + ε

What b buys you: if b = −0.15 percentage points per year, then extending from 4 to 10 years of term tightens the cap by roughly 90bp — on a $2,000,000 asset at a 6.0% cap, that is a value change of roughly $330,000 from a lease amendment alone. That is the calculation that decides whether to renew before selling.

Honest limit: with eight to twelve observations the standard errors will be wide, and the result should be treated as a range, not a point. The value is in the sign and rough magnitude, not in a precise coefficient. Anyone quoting a precise number from a sample that small is overfitting.

4 · What the tenant's own filings imply about the renewal ask

The tenant has both the ability and the incentive to renew at a moderate rent

Three verified facts from the FY2025 10-K frame the negotiation:

  • Lease capital costs the tenant about 6.1% on a weighted-average basis — its disclosed operating lease discount rate.
  • Its average remaining term is 11.2 years — so the tenant is accustomed to long commitments and has a revealed preference for them.
  • Its franchise rental revenue line is stable — $368.6m in FY2025 against $375.4m and $364.6m in the two prior years, inside a company whose total revenue fell from $1.69bn to $1.47bn over the same period.

Read together: the real-estate side of this tenant's business is its most stable segment while the operating side contracts. That is an argument that a renewal is achievable — the tenant values lease stability — and also that the landlord should not expect an above-market rent, because total revenue is shrinking and impairment charges have been large ($209.6m in FY2025, $162.6m in FY2024).

Why active listings cannot substitute for closed sales. A listing cap rate is what a seller hopes to achieve. A closed cap rate is what a buyer actually paid, after negotiation and due diligence. The gap between them is systematically in one direction, and it widens exactly when the market is soft — which is the state in which a seller most needs an accurate number.

6 · Using the discount to choose the path

A decision table that does not require a cap rate to be assumed

Once the term coefficient b is estimated, the sell-versus-renew question becomes an arithmetic comparison rather than a judgement call:

Compare the cost of waiting against the value of the extension
PathValue todayValue after extensionChoose it when
Sell now, at current term NOI ÷ cap(termnow) — When the extension value is smaller than the cost, risk and time of negotiating it.
Renew first, then sell — NOI ÷ cap(termextended) − renewal costs − rent concession When b is large enough that the cap-rate tightening exceeds the concession plus the delay.
Hold to expiry, then re-let — Market rent ÷ cap(market) − downtime − TI − commissions Only when the site's land value or re-leasing prospects clearly exceed the leased-fee value.

Every input in the middle column is currently unknown. The table is published as the structure of the decision, not as its answer.
