Five decision options, their risks, and a calculator that runs entirely in your browser. The calculator computes; it does not advise, and it will not produce a verdict while the inputs it needs are blank.

No winner is selected on this page, by instruction and by evidence. The brief asks for an after-tax sale comparison alongside a negotiated renewal case without picking a winner until verified inputs exist. They do not exist. What exists is the machinery to compare them the moment the inputs do.

How to read the output honestly

  • The comparison is net after-tax sale proceeds today versus the present value of the renewal case. Those are not the same kind of number, and the page says so rather than subtracting one from the other.
  • The calculator will still run with blank or zero inputs. That is deliberate: it shows you a number built on nothing, which is what a rule-of-thumb valuation often is.
  • The single most sensitive input is the cap rate. A 50 basis point move on a 5.75% cap is roughly a 9% change in value before any income change.
  • If the sale price is unknown, the correct output is 'unknown', not a guess. The calculator cannot enforce that; you have to.

Price the short-lease discount from real quotes

The brief asks to price the short-lease discount from real quotes. That is the single most important number in the sale case, because the lease expires inside a five-year window and a buyer prices the reversion as a re-leasing risk rather than as income. It cannot be derived from a rule of thumb without becoming an assumption.

What it takes. Pairs of otherwise-similar single-tenant quick-service trades in the same submarket, one with a long remaining term and one short, sold close in time, each with the cap rate and the term remaining at sale. The discount is then the observed cap-rate spread between the two groups, expressed in basis points, and it should be re-read every time a new pair trades.

What is shown here. Nothing, because no such pairs have been documented in this workspace. The row is on the Flagged for Review page as FR-09, with the reason: no official agency publishes commercial cap rates or lease comparables, so this must come from a broker's documented file with source documents attached.

Scenarios — explicitly labelled as assumptions

The brief asks to distinguish observations from assumptions. Everything below is an assumption. None of it is used as a value on any other page. It exists to name the branches a decision has to survive, not to forecast.

Business as usual SC-1

Premise. Tenant performs through expiry, renews or is replaced at or near market, cap rates hold, no capital event larger than routine maintenance.

What changes. Rent grows on the lease schedule; property tax grows no faster than the Proposition 13 cap of 2% absent a change of ownership.

Assumption, not finding. That the tenant renews. That is an assumption, not a fact, and it is the single most important one in the file.

What would disconfirm it. A notice of non-renewal, or inclusion of the unit in a closure programme.

Rate and cap-rate normalisation SC-2

Premise. Cap rates move against the owner because long rates rise or because lenders price short-lease single-tenant risk wider.

What changes. Value moves inversely with the cap rate. A 50 basis point move on a 5.50% cap is roughly an 8% value change before any income change.

Assumption, not finding. The direction and size of the cap-rate move. Nothing in this repository supports a specific number, which is why the calculator asks for it.

What would disconfirm it. Closed comparable trades at stable cap rates.

Tenant credit event SC-3

Premise. The operator stops paying or vacates before expiry, inside a soft leasing market.

What changes. Rent stops, the owner carries taxes, insurance and security, and the site may need re-tenanting capex.

Assumption, not finding. The vacancy duration and re-tenanting cost. Both are user inputs in the calculator.

What would disconfirm it. A confirmed renewal, or a guaranty from a stronger obligor than assumed.

Black swan: regional catastrophe SC-4

Premise. A damaging earthquake, or a wildfire-driven regional insurance and power event, closes the unit for a period and reprices insurance across the region.

What changes. Loss of rent for the closure period, repair cost net of insurance, a higher premium afterwards, and a thinner buyer pool at exit.

Assumption, not finding. The closure length, the uninsured share, and the premium shock. No official source supplies these for this parcel, and they are inputs, not findings.

What would disconfirm it. A parcel-specific hazard screen showing no special-flood or high-fire designation plus confirmed earthquake cover with a serviceable deductible.

Black swan: Los Angeles-specific shock outside the property SC-5

Premise. A regional event that does not damage the building but damages demand: a prolonged entertainment or port disruption, a large employer exit, or a sustained out-migration.

What changes. Trade-area sales fall, tenant renewal appetite falls, and the cap rate a buyer will pay widens even if the building is intact.

Assumption, not finding. That such an event occurs and its magnitude. Not modelled numerically; it is a qualitative branch on the Risk page.

What would disconfirm it. Sustained employment growth with sector diversification. The verified BLS data currently show exactly that pattern in part: health and education +3.8% offsetting information -5.7%.

Wealth preservation and growth: the balance the brief asks for

The case for a lower-risk path

A single tenant, a single parcel and a lease ending inside five years is a concentrated position with a dated cliff. If the owner's objective includes wealth preservation, concentration plus a known expiry is the risk to reduce. A sale converts an illiquid, single-obligor position into cash or into a diversified replacement, and it removes the option-window risk entirely.

But: a sale now realises the gain and pays the tax, and the tax rate depends on facts (basis, depreciation, state of residence) that are not yet in hand. Selling before those are known is itself a risk.

The case for keeping the income

The verified local evidence does not support a deteriorating market: metro unemployment fell to 4.4%, employment is growing, and the health and education sector that anchors weekday lunch traffic added 49,100 jobs in a year. Meanwhile California house prices grew only 0.6% over four quarters, so there is no evident price bubble forcing a sale.

But: wages in the Los Angeles CSA rose 3.9% against local CPI of 3.7%, and the tenant's labour cost is set by statute. A tenant whose cost base grows faster than its sales is a tenant that negotiates rent hard, which is the reason the renewal case needs a market rent before it can be argued.

The balanced answer the evidence supports. Reduce the irreversible risks first, and do not make an irreversible commitment until the three numbers exist. Concretely: close the lease and notice-calendar gate, get the basis from the CPA, and get three dated comparables. Those three steps are inexpensive, they are mutually independent, and each one removes a way the decision can go wrong. Everything on this page is built to be used the moment they are done.
